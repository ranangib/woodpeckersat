The grammar guide

Review the sentence structure and standard English conventions that help you make sense of grammar questions. Learn a reliable way to analyze the sentence, apply the rule, and check that your choice preserves the intended meaning.

Start here: Find the sentence’s subject and main verb. Decide whether each side of the blank is a complete thought. Then choose punctuation or a word that connects those ideas correctly.

1. Clauses, phrases, and sentences

A clause has a subject and a verb. A phrase is a group of words that works together but does not contain a complete subject-and-verb unit.

Clause: the rain stopped (subject rain; verb stopped)

Phrase: after the rain (no subject-verb pair)

Independent and dependent clauses

An independent clause expresses a complete thought and can stand as a sentence. A dependent clause has a subject and verb but begins or ends in a way that leaves the thought incomplete. Words such as because, although, when, if, and while often introduce dependent clauses.

The team continued its work — independent clause.

although the deadline was close — dependent clause.

Combined: Although the deadline was close, the team continued its work.

A simple sentence has one independent clause. A compound sentence joins independent clauses. A complex sentence combines an independent clause with at least one dependent clause. A compound-complex sentence has both combinations.

2. Sentence boundaries and run-ons

Every independent clause needs a correct connection to the clause next to it. A fused sentence joins two independent clauses with no punctuation; a comma splice joins them with only a comma.

Fused: The library was closing we packed our books.

Comma splice: The library was closing, we packed our books.

Period: The library was closing. We packed our books.

Semicolon: The library was closing; we packed our books.

Comma + coordinating conjunction: The library was closing, so we packed our books.

A comma alone cannot join two complete sentences. To check, read the words on each side of the proposed punctuation and ask whether each side can stand alone.

3. Commas and supplementary information

Use a comma in a series of three or more

Keep items in a list parallel, and use the comma before the final and or or when separating three or more items.

The lab measured temperature, pressure, and humidity.

Do not add a comma just because you see and: The lab measured temperature and pressure.

Use a comma between independent clauses joined by FANBOYS

FANBOYS stands for for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. Use a comma before the conjunction when it joins two independent clauses. If the second part is not an independent clause, the comma is usually not needed.

The sample was small, but the result was consistent.

The sample was small but consistent. (No second independent clause.)

Separate introductory elements and nonessential information

Use a comma after an introductory phrase or dependent clause. Set off an interruption or nonessential information with a matching pair of commas. Essential information identifies which person or thing is meant and should not be separated from that noun with commas.

After reviewing the data, the researchers revised their estimate.

The telescope, which was recently repaired, is operating again.

The telescope that was recently repaired is operating again. (Identifies which telescope.)

Use the removal test for a possible interruption: temporarily remove the words. If the main sentence remains clear and the information is extra, commas may be appropriate. If removing it changes who or what the sentence identifies, it is essential.

Commas with dates, places, and direct address

When a full date or a city-and-state name appears in the middle of a sentence, close the pair with a comma. A comma can also separate a person’s name in direct address.

On April 8, 2024, the observatory opened to visitors.

The festival in Salem, Oregon, attracts local artists.

Mina, please review the final paragraph.

4. Periods, semicolons, and colons

Periods

Use a period to separate two independent clauses into separate sentences. Each clause must be able to stand alone as a complete sentence.

The forecast changed. The expedition moved indoors.

Semicolons

A semicolon works much like a period: it can separate two independent clauses. Use one when the sentences are somewhat related. The words on both sides must be able to stand alone as complete sentences.

The forecast changed; the expedition moved indoors.

The forecast changed; moving the expedition indoors.

When a conjunctive adverb such as however, therefore, consequently, moreover, or nevertheless connects two independent clauses, use a semicolon before it and a comma after it.

The route was longer than expected; however, the group arrived on time.

The route was longer than expected, however, the group arrived on time.

Semicolons can also separate complex list items when those items already contain commas.

The panel included Hana Lee, a biologist; Ravi Shah, an engineer; and Lila Chen, a historian.

Colons

Use a colon after a complete independent clause to introduce a list, example, or explanation. Do not put a colon directly between a verb or preposition and its complement.

The field kit contains three tools: a compass, a ruler, and a notebook.

The field kit contains: a compass, a ruler, and a notebook.

A colon may also join two independent clauses when the second explains or illustrates the first. One useful check: does the second clause answer why? or explain the first?

The observatory closed early: a storm was approaching.

5. Conjunctions and logical relationships

Grammar is not only about punctuation. A connector must express the relationship between the ideas: addition, contrast, cause, result, or condition. Choose the transition that matches the meaning, rather than one that merely sounds formal.

Addition: The method is inexpensive, and it is easy to repeat.

Contrast: The method is inexpensive, but it takes more time.

Result: The method is inexpensive; therefore, more schools can use it.

Use coordination when ideas have similar importance, and subordination when one idea is background or less important. A subordinating word such as because, although, when, or if makes its clause dependent.

The archive was closed, so the class visited the museum.

Because the archive was closed, the class visited the museum.

Keep list items and paired constructions in matching grammatical forms. For example, pair to measure with to compare, or measuring with comparing.

6. Subject-verb and pronoun agreement

Find the true subject before choosing the verb

A verb agrees with its subject, not with a nearby noun inside a prepositional phrase or interruption. Mentally set aside phrases beginning with words such as of, with, near, and between to identify the core subject.

One of the instruments are missing.

One of the instruments is missing. (Subject: one.)

The instruments in the case are missing. (Subject: instruments.)

Compound subjects joined by and usually take a plural verb. With sentences beginning there is or there are, find the subject after the verb and match the verb to it. Also check sentences with inverted word order: the subject may come after the verb.

There are several reasons for the change.

Near the entrance stands a row of ancient trees. (Subject: row.)

Make pronouns agree with clear antecedents

A pronoun refers to a noun called its antecedent. Keep their number consistent, and make sure the reference is clear. Singular antecedents such as each and neither need a singular pronoun in formal test questions.

Each seedling needs its own label.

The seedlings need their own labels.

When two possible nouns could be the antecedent, revise so the pronoun’s reference is unmistakable.

7. Verb tense and form

Keep verb tense consistent unless the timing of events changes. Use time clues such as dates and phrases like by the time to establish when an action occurs. The past perfect (had + past participle) can mark an action that happened before another past action.

The team tested the design and records the results.

The team tested the design and recorded the results.

By the time the guide arrived, the group had left.

When discussing what happens in a work of literature, present tense is the usual convention.

In the final chapter, the narrator reveals the reason for the journey.

Check the verb itself as well as its tense: the subject and verb must agree, and the form after a helping verb must be standard (for example, has written, not has wrote).

8. Modifiers and prepositional phrases

A modifier describes another word or phrase. Place it close enough to the thing it describes that readers can tell what it modifies. A dangling modifier has no logical word to describe.

While hiking the trail, the clouds covered the peak.

While hiking the trail, we saw clouds cover the peak.

She served sandwiches to the hikers on paper plates. (This can sound like the hikers are on plates.)

She served the hikers sandwiches on paper plates.

An introductory modifying phrase should describe the subject that follows it. Prepositional phrases also contain nouns that can distract from the sentence’s actual subject; do not let those nearby nouns control the verb.

9. Apostrophes and commonly confused words

Possession and contractions

Use an apostrophe to show possession or missing letters in a contraction—not to make a noun plural. Most singular possessives take ’s; plural nouns ending in s usually take only an apostrophe.

One researcher: the researcher’s notes

Several researchers: the researchers’ notes

Contraction: it’s = it is; possessive: its = belonging to it

Contraction: they’re = they are; possessive: their

Try expanding a contraction to check it: if it is does not make sense, use its. Similarly, you’re means you are; your is possessive.

Commonly confused words

  • affect / effect: usually, affect is a verb meaning influence; effect is a noun meaning result.
  • their / there / they’re: possession / place or existence / they are.
  • whose / who’s: possession / who is or who has.
  • precede / proceed: come before / continue or move forward.
  • accept / except: receive / excluding.

For idiomatic expressions, choose the standard preposition that fits the phrase’s meaning (for example, depend on and different from). In test questions, use the context and the answer choices rather than changing wording without a reason.

10. A step-by-step test method

  1. Decide whether the question tests punctuation or subject-verb agreement. The answer choices often reveal which skill is being tested.
  2. For subject-verb agreement, identify the subject and main verb, then apply the agreement rules above and the rules you already know.
  3. For a sentence-boundary question, cross out nonessential phrases in the passage. Then look at the words before and after the punctuation point and decide whether each side is an independent or dependent clause.
  4. Use the relevant rules above to choose the punctuation that correctly joins or separates the clauses.
  5. Read your selected answer back into the sentence. Confirm that it is grammatical and preserves the intended relationship between ideas.

When choices differ only in punctuation, the before-and-after check is a useful first step. If either side is not an independent clause, neither a semicolon nor a period is appropriate for joining the two sides; look for a clause, phrase, or supplementary-information rule instead.
